# Club Penguin playtest: a new player's first session

I played **New Club Penguin** (newcp.net, a fan recreation of the 2013–2016 client) as a brand-new penguin, `testmayank3`, on 2026-10-17. You created and signed in to the account; I took over from the first room. My background research on the original game (sources and dates) is in [club-penguin-desk-research.md](club-penguin-desk-research.md). Earlier research on the room list, map and art style is in [research.md](research.md).

**Caveats.** New Club Penguin is a recreation. Where it differs from Disney's original I say so; for example, its newspaper button opens a blog. I could only play through the in-app browser pane at about 500 px wide, so I couldn't read small tooltip text. I also couldn't play real-time drag games properly: the Pizzatron sauce needs a held, sweeping drag.

## 2. Findings by area

### UI design
- **Fixed 760×480 stage** (the canvas is 1520×960, which is 2× that), scaled to fit. The HUD sits **inside** the art: a rounded blue bar along the bottom, with the map as a separate tilted "folded map" icon in the bottom-left corner. Top-left has the newspaper and mail; top-right has mod/help and currency.
- **Toolbar order** (left → right): [puffle] · emote · actions · snowball · Safe Chat · *chat field* · send · player card · friends · igloo · settings. Expression goes on the left, people and places on the right.
- **Every menu is a popup anchored to its button** (emotes, actions, Safe Chat cascade), or a **docked side panel** (player card left, inventory sliding out right). **Full-screen takeovers** are kept for games, the catalog and mail. That gives three clear levels of interruption.
- **Visual language:** one blue, chunky rounded corners, a thick white stroke around panels, round icon buttons, yellow tooltips and labels, Burbank-style bold type. Modals read like plain speech: a question, then **Yes / No**.
- **Skeuomorphism where it adds delight:** the mail is a desk, the catalog is a magazine with page curls, likes use an odometer, the map is a postcard. Everything else is flat and fast.
- **Every loading screen is themed** to where you're going (pizza toss, chef). Even waiting is part of the world.

### Layout and level design
- Each room is **one screen** with a clear "hero" building or prop, a big open walkable floor in the middle, and exits around the edges. Shops have one **center-stage set piece** (the catwalk, the stage, the piano).
- Games live **inside rooms behind a physical door or object** (the kitchen door), not in a menu. The map's Games filter is the shortcut, and it hands off with an arrow.
- Room-specific HUD buttons (catalog in shops, edit and catalog at home) keep the global bar small.

### Game design (mini-games)
- The same five-part shell every time: **confirm → title card → comic instructions → play → payout card**. You can quit at any moment and still get paid for what you did.
- The payout card is the real loop: **coins → stamps you haven't earned yet → three suggestions for spending**. From the desk research: payout is about 10% of score, and **2× once you own all of that game's stamps**.
- Difficulty comes from a **long, fixed run** (40 pizzas) with mistakes that build up, not from lives running out. That's gentle for kids.

### Social mechanics
- **The player card is the social hub.** Click anyone and see their whole look and status, plus a fixed action row: add friend · find · visit igloo · postcard · stamps · ignore · report.
- **Menu chat (Safe Chat)** covers a surprising amount: greetings, questions, game invites, *"Can I have a tour?"*, and an anti-bullying "Grub!" branch. It looks exactly like typed chat.
- Showing off is the social glue: outfits, puffles, card backgrounds and igloo likes are all **visible to everyone**.
- **The emptiness problem is real.** The live crowd collapsed into one welcome room, and every other room I visited had 0–3 people. The original fixed this with 300-player servers and a server picker that shows how busy each one is. Club NYC's single shared world plus city locals is the right instinct; the next step is to *nudge players toward where the people are* (see gaps below).

### Creativity and art
- Every surface rewards a closer look: the duck in the pond, the picnic blanket, Fred the cuckoo clock (a loading-screen fun fact tells you he pops out every 30 minutes).
- Seasonal reskins reach the login screen, the catalog cover and the map. On 2026-10-17 the server was mid-"Food Fair" party with a Halloween catalog.

### Product and economy
- Start with **500 coins**, and the welcome postcard explains why. The first useful purchases are **20-coin colors** and **25-coin hats**, so a single game round already buys something.
- **Browse everything, gate only at Buy** (the original's membership wall). You confirm at the moment of purchase, with your balance shown.
- The calendar does the retention work: a weekly paper, a monthly catalog, a monthly party, pins every 2 weeks (desk research §4).

### Safety and moderation (from the desk research; the recreation's rules page says the same)
- Allowed-word chat (about 300k words), **no digits**, 48 characters, blocked messages **silently dropped**.
- Name shown as `P########` until a moderator approves it.
- Ban ladder: 24 h / 72 h / permanent.
- Kids recruited as "Secret Agent" reporters, with monthly coin paychecks.
- A server-wide **menu-only kill switch** that was used once, in 2010.

## 3. Compared with Club NYC (checked against the code today)

**Already matched:** fixed stage plus map fast travel; emotes and actions on keys; quick chat; player card; catalog with **hover try-on** (`features/catalog.ts`, "Hover an item to try it on"); a game payout card with Play again / Leaderboard / Collect (`games/host.ts`); apartment with likes and guestbook; stamps; Room 2 overflow at 60.

**Gaps worth stealing**, roughly in order of payoff:
1. **"Use your coins to…" on the payout card.** Three buttons (outfits / home / [pet or bodega]) that take you straight to the shop. Club NYC's card ends at "Collect".
2. **Map filters (Places / Games / Shops) plus an in-room arrow** pointing at the thing you chose. `features/map.ts` has no filters today.
3. **Point players at the crowd.** The original's server picker showed how busy each server was. Club NYC equivalent: crowd dots or "12 people here" on the map and subway map, and a "Go where people are" button.
4. **Show the stamps you haven't earned on the payout card** (the grey 0/10 row) so every round shows what's still to collect.
5. **Confirm before a game starts** ("Want to play Slice Factory? Yes / No") when walking onto a game spot, if that isn't already how the job lobby opens.
6. **Themed loading screens** for each game or room.
7. **A card-backdrop slot**: a cheap 60-coin cosmetic that shows on every profile view.
8. **A pending-name state** (like CP's `P12345`) and a **menu-only chat kill switch** on the server for incidents.
9. **Errand-style first day** (the original's 2013 guide sent you to the Clothes Shop, Pet Shop and your igloo, with an exclusive item at each). Rosa's tour already does something like this; check that each stop gives an item that can't be bought later.

## 4. What I'd research next to replicate it exactly

- **Frame-by-frame capture** at full resolution (a desktop browser, not the narrow pane): every toolbar tooltip, hover and pressed states, panel open/close timing, bubble lifetime, walk speed in px/s, and how far you can click outside the walkable area.
- **A second account** in the same room to test buddy requests, "find", igloo visits, postcards and how the report flow looks to the other player.
- **Play each mini-game to the end** to record payout curves (score → coins) and stamp triggers. That needs a human at the mouse for drag games.
- **A day-7 revisit** to see what changes for a returning player (new catalog, party, mail, pins) compared with day 1.
- **Read the original client's room SWFs** (from the fan archives) to measure walkable areas, trigger zones and depth layers precisely.
- **Watch real players for 30 minutes in the busiest room**: what they say, how groups form and break up, how often they use menu chat versus typing, where they stand.
